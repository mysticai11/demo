Predictive Risk Intelligence for Metabolic Screening in Diabetes

LMSIS — Latent Metabolic State Inference System

A deep generative model that recovers hidden metabolic risk from routine blood biomarkers in adults with a normal body weight.

View on GitHub Read the Full Report Try the Live Demo

Millions of adults with a clinically normal body weight may carry hidden, concurrent insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis — a combination of metabolic dysfunction that routine screening, built around Body Mass Index, is structurally unable to detect. LMSIS is a semi-supervised deep generative model that learns a two-dimensional metabolic coordinate space directly from fourteen routine blood biomarkers, with each axis mathematically anchored to a real clinical reference measurement: insulin resistance and liver fat content. The system places every patient on this map, provides a calibrated confidence range specific to their risk group, and computes a route back toward a metabolically healthy position — translated into the exact biomarker changes that would move them there.

+ Technical details

The architecture is a Dual-Anchored Semi-Supervised Identifiable Variational Autoencoder (DA-SS-iVAE). Identifiability follows from conditioning the latent prior on demographic covariates, per Khemakhem et al. (2020). Each anchor network is constrained to strictly positive weights via a Softplus reprojection, guaranteeing monotonicity between each coordinate and its clinical target. The steatosis anchor trains semi-supervised through a binary availability mask, since imaging-derived labels exist only for a subset of participants. Per-subgroup uncertainty uses Mondrian conformal prediction, stratified by phenotypic quadrant, directly addressing the subgroup coverage impossibility result of Barber et al. (2023). Intervention pathways are geodesics on the Riemannian manifold induced by the decoder Jacobian, projected back to biomarker space.

Questions this work is likely to raise — answered here

Is this system ready to be used on real patients?

No. This is a research prototype intended to demonstrate technical feasibility, not a diagnostic device. It has not undergone clinical trials, regulatory review, or prospective validation, and using it to direct real patient care would be inappropriate at this stage.

How is this different from a doctor simply reviewing a normal blood panel?

A doctor typically checks each value against its own reference range independently. The hidden risk this system targets often shows up only as a combination — several values sitting just inside their individual normal ranges at once, in a pattern that signals dysfunction collectively even though none of them individually crosses a threshold. The model reads all fourteen values together as a single pattern, which single-value screening cannot do.

Why not just use a simpler, off-the-shelf model like Random Forest?

Tree-based models trained on the same data achieve a comparable or marginally higher raw correlation with liver fat. But they produce only a single number with no further structure: no biologically meaningful coordinate system, no per-subgroup confidence guarantee, and no way to extract an explanation of how the prediction was reached. At the clinical decision threshold that matters, this system achieves higher classification performance than those simpler models, while also providing structure a single-output model cannot.

How do you know the two coordinates actually mean insulin resistance and liver fat, rather than being arbitrary numbers?

Two safeguards are built into the architecture. Each coordinate passes through a monotone-constrained network trained against a real, independently measured clinical value, so it is mathematically forced to increase exactly when that measurement increases. The underlying model is also conditioned on patient demographics specifically to satisfy a published identifiability theorem, which prevents the coordinate axes from being an arbitrary, unrepeatable rotation.

How do you know the confidence ranges are trustworthy, especially for the highest-risk patients?

Standard uncertainty methods are calibrated to the population on average, which is precisely what allows them to quietly fail an above-average-risk minority — a known mathematical limitation, not a flaw specific to this dataset. This system calibrates uncertainty separately within each risk subgroup, which is shown to restore reliable coverage specifically where the standard approach fails.

Could this just be overfit to one dataset?

It was evaluated on a separate group of patients collected in a later, independent survey cycle the model never saw during training, and separately on a demographic subgroup outside its primary training composition. Performance held up in both cases, which is the standard test for whether a pattern generalizes rather than memorizes.

Why is the national prevalence estimate given with such a wide range?

That width honestly reflects how few patients in a national survey fall into this specific, narrowly defined subgroup — a property of the underlying sample size, not a flaw in the model. The estimate is never reported without that range, because the range is the more scientifically meaningful number.

What is the single biggest limitation of this work right now?

Every patient in the dataset was measured exactly once. The system has not yet been shown to predict what happens to a real patient over time, only that it can recover a snapshot of their current hidden state. Tracking real patients forward is the most important next step.

A generative model whose internal coordinates are mathematically anchored to real, independently verifiable clinical measurements, rather than left as an unexplainable black box.

A calibration method that restores reliable confidence estimates specifically for the highest-risk patient subgroup, where standard methods are shown to fail.

An interpretability layer that extracts plain mathematical formulas describing how the model's internal coordinates relate to real biomarkers.

A complete, tested, end-to-end research system — from data pipeline through model to an interactive clinical interface.

What we don't yet know

  • All data comes from a single national health survey.
  • Every measurement is a one-time snapshot, not tracked over time.
  • This is a research prototype, not a diagnostic device.

What's next

  • Validation on a fully independent, separately collected cohort.
  • Tracking real patients over time to test predictive value.
  • Expanding validation across a wider range of population groups.
